I didn't write a login system. I hired one.

Nine desks, one building, and the secret to shipping a working service in a weekend: build only the part nobody else can build for you.

Alan Munro · November 2026

Two businesses and one pink bird

First, why a newsletter app at all. Flockletter is the third app I've built with AI. The first two are called Cadence and Frosty Flamingo Apparel, and each one is getting its own episode, so that's all you get today. The only spoiler you need: one of them sells a hoodie, and I had no way to tell anybody the hoodie existed.

The other reason is one you've already met. If you read the original series, you know Frosty, the agent that helps run our short-term rental in Keystone, Colorado. Ski season is opening. There are past guests who would love an excuse to come back, and I had no polite, permission-based way to hand them one.

So Flockletter's job description wrote itself. Let someone sign up for the updates they want. Let me build a newsletter with the actual photos. Remember, precisely, who asked for what. The first issue went to the Keystone list.

Then I kept going, because building a newsletter app on a plane is my idea of a good time and I'm aware it isn't everyone's. Most shop owners just want to send the newsletter. So Flockletter is a hosted service too: sign up, add your photos, write your first issue, and once delivery is active on your plan, pick your audience and hit send. And because it's open source, anyone who does share my idea of a good time can read every line of it or run their own.

The org chart, again

Part 1 of the original series was an org chart, and it turns out an app is one too. I didn't write a login system, a payment system, or a mail server, and neither did ChatGPT. I hired for those desks. Scroll back up to the chart and here's who's sitting where.

  • GitHub, chief of staff. All the code lives there, every change runs the full test suite there, and nothing reaches production except through its release button.
  • Render, the building. It hosts the web app, a background worker, and the database, once for staging and once for production, with separate logins and separate data.
  • PostgreSQL, the records room. Who signed up for what, when, through which form, and who asked to stop. Every issue, every approval.
  • Clerk, the front desk. It checks ID. I never see a password.
  • Stripe, accounts. Plans and payments. I never see a card number.
  • Listmonk, the print shop. The open-source engine from the terminal train. It assembles each newsletter.
  • Resend, the mail room. It carries the mail out and reports back when an address bounces.
  • Cloudflare, the switchboard. It runs the DNS, which is how a browser finds the site and how an inbox can tell the mail is really ours.
  • The workshop. The Mac Studio at home and ChatGPT, where the code gets written before any of the above ever sees it.

There's also a side entrance for scripts and AI agents, which answers to the same rules as the front door. An agent can't do anything through it that a person couldn't do through the website.

What was left for me to build

Take away everything rented and what's left is the middle of the chart, which is the actual product. It's almost entirely rules.

  • Permission is a record, not an assumption. Every subscriber has a paper trail: where they signed up, when, and for what. Signup forms confirm by email before anyone is added, by default.
  • Each brand keeps its own list. Unsubscribing from hoodie news doesn't unsubscribe you from ski season, and signing up for one never signs you up for the other.
  • A purchase is not consent. Buying a hoodie does not put you on a mailing list.
  • Approval means this exact version. If the newsletter changes after I approve it, the approval doesn't carry over.
  • Check again before it leaves. That one gets its own episode.

Why rent the hard parts

It's the same call I'd make at work. You don't build your own power plant to run a headend. Logins, payments and mail delivery are each somebody's full-time specialty, with security teams I can't match from a kitchen table. Renting them meant the weekend went into the only part that's actually mine: deciding what I actually want to build.

Which brings me to the one rule I care about most, and the test that proves it. That's Part 3.
