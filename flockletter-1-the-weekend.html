The Staff of Ten · Flockletter · Part 1 of 3

I went to see my son and came home with an app

Flockletter was designed in an airport security line, coded overnight while I slept, and debugged at a Renaissance festival. It is also my third app, which is something I didn't have on my bingo card this year.

Alan Munro · November 2026

Flockletter is live. Real site, real logins, real newsletters: flockletter.frostyflamingosoftware.com. It's an open-source newsletter service for small businesses, the kind with a few hundred people who actually asked to hear from them and an owner who would rather be doing anything other than configuring email.

A week ago it was a requirements document on a tray table.

I flew to Oklahoma City to visit my son and daughter-in-law. We had a great time. I also learned how much software fits into the gaps of a trip: the shuttle, the line, the train, the gate. My wife was with me the whole way, and she is a patient woman. The minutes I'd normally lose to scrolling my phone turned into an app.

The itinerary was the roadmap

The research came first, by voice, going back and forth with ChatGPT about how newsletter services are actually put together. Then the decisions started landing, roughly one per mode of transportation.

Who handles logins got settled on the shuttle bus. Who handles payments got settled in the security line, somewhere around the bins. Which open-source newsletter engine to build on top of got settled on the train to the concourse. I finished the requirements on the plane, spotty wifi and all, and the build had already started before we pushed back from the gate.

Write it down or it didn't happen

Here's the habit that made the rest possible. Every one of those decisions went into writing the moment it was made. Not in my head, not in a chat I'd have to scroll back through. In the project itself: a requirements document where every requirement has a number, and a decision log where every choice has a number, a date, and a note on what it replaced.

That sounds like paperwork, and it is. It's the same paperwork I'd want from a vendor at work. The difference is that this time the reader wasn't an auditor. The reader was the thing doing the building, and it reads everything, every time, without skimming.

The night shift

By the time I landed, ChatGPT was in full coding mode.

And did it code. It coded all night, and it finished up just in time for breakfast.

That wasn't luck, and it wasn't magic. With every decision from the commute memorialized, and a bit of tuning on my prompts, it never had to stop and ask what I meant. I'd already said so. So I went to bed, and the work kept going without me. I have managed night shifts before. This was the first one that didn't call.

Standing orders

The prompt tuning mostly turned into an instruction file that lives in the project. It grew over the week. A few of its rules, lightly paraphrased:

  • Make reasonable decisions without checking in on routine ones.
  • Keep working while I'm away. Save the real questions and ask them together.
  • Anything that costs more than $25, stop and ask. No splitting a purchase to sneak under.
  • Record every decision, and the evidence for it.
  • Building something is not permission to turn it on. Real email and real charges each need their own yes from me.

Read that list as a manager and it's familiar. It's a delegation memo: here's your authority, here's your spending limit, here's what you escalate. The AI part is new. The memo isn't.

Debugging, with armor

The debugging happened in daylight. Saturday I was at the Oklahoma City Renaissance festival, and between rounds at the combat arena, while grown men in armor caught their breath, I spent a few minutes with ChatGPT chasing a bug. By Sunday afternoon I could put together a newsletter with my own photos and rehearse exactly what would happen when it went out.

One week of hardening later, it's in production.

That's the weekend. But "ChatGPT built an app overnight" is the least interesting way to tell it, because most of what makes Flockletter work wasn't built at all. It was hired. That's Part 2.
